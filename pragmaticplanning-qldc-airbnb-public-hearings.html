Airbnb Crackdown: Public Hearings Underway. Time for a Q & A.

Public hearings on the proposed new Queenstown Lakes District short-term accommodation rules commenced on 4 September 2018. Here is a Q&A covering who makes the decision, what happens to resource consent applications, the apartment rules, and what property owners needed to do.

[TL;DR]
  • QLDC proposed reducing non-consent letting from 90 to 28 nights/year
  • Council's own evidence recommended relaxation to 42 nights/year
  • Maximum 3 individual lets/year proposed under new rules
  • Resource consent granted before new rules applied = rights locked in
  • Apartments require consent: cannot use the 90-night permitted allowance
  • For 2026 current rules, see the updated rules page

Archived Content (2019 Update): The information on this page reflects the position as at September 2018. It is retained for reference purposes only. For the current rules following the 2023 Environment Court decision, please see: Council's New Short-Term Visitor Accommodation Rules.

Nine Months On: A Recap

Public hearings on Stream 15 of the Queenstown Lakes District Plan Review commenced on Tuesday 4 September 2018. The most anticipated and contentious topic was the proposed replacement rules around using residential properties for short-term accommodation.

Here is a recap of what QLDC formally proposed: reducing the permitted number of nights for short-term accommodation without resource consent from 90 to 28, and introducing a maximum of just 3 individual lets within that 28 nights. Applying for more than this would require a "non-complying" activity resource consent in most residential areas, which would be difficult to obtain. More context can be found in our earlier posts on the Airbnb crackdown.

The Q & A

Has Council Changed Their Mind Going Into the Hearings?

A bit. In Council's evidence to their Independent Hearings Panel, they recommended a very slight relaxation: 42 nights per annum permitted, but more than 42 nights still requiring a "non-complying" activity resource consent, which would be likely difficult to obtain.

Who Makes the Decision?

Independent Commissioners were appointed to hear the matter at public hearings from 4 September to 28 September 2018. After the hearings, they would deliberate and provide the Council with a recommendation. The Council would then usually publicly notify that recommendation without changes, at which point new rules would begin to have legal effect.

How Long Is This Likely to Take?

After the hearings ended on 28 September, the Commissioners would deliberate and write recommended decision reports. The exact timeframe was not known, but based on the time taken for decisions in the previous set of hearings, this could be anywhere from 1.5 to several months.

What Happens if I Apply for Resource Consent Before Decisions Are Made?

If you applied for and were granted a resource consent for short-term accommodation before decisions were made, your application was assessed under the current, more permissive rules. You could then continue to use your property for short-term accommodation in future, in accordance with the conditions of that consent, even if the rules changed. This is why there was a significant flood of resource consent applications throughout 2018: many property owners were attempting to get in and secure their position.

What Happens if I Apply for Resource Consent After New Rules Are Made?

If you applied for a resource consent after the decisions were made, the new rules (whatever they may be) would begin to have legal effect. At that point there was no way of knowing exactly how Council would treat resource consent applications under the new framework.

Can I Be Heard at the Public Hearings?

Usually only if you had made a submission or were called as a witness. It was strongly recommended to take professional advice before considering this.

What Evidence Did Council Have?

Council's evidence, including economic evidence, was available on the Council's website at the time of the hearings.

Can I Just Keep Operating Under the 90-Day Rule if I Have Always Been Complying?

Possibly. This is a complex area of legal resource management law called "existing use rights." However, there are several significant downsides: the inability to change the nature or scale of the activity; the requirement not to pause the activity for more than 12 months; the risk that changes to the house may extinguish any existing use rights; and the fact that the onus of proof lies with the landowner. This is another reason hundreds of property owners applied for a resource consent or certificate of compliance in 2018.

[Important: Apartments and Townhouses]

A Common Misconception

This is a big one. You cannot operate as a Registered Holiday Home (that is, use "the 90 nights" permitted allowance) if your property is not a free-standing dwelling or duplex of two units. Pretty much all apartments require a resource consent for short-term accommodation, and many were likely to be caught by any new rules, simply because owners had not realised this subtle difference.

If you are using an apartment or townhouse (one of three or more attached units) for any short-term accommodation and wish to do so in future, you need a resource consent. If you wanted your application assessed under the existing rules, applying as soon as possible was critical.

Do I Need Professional Holiday Home Management?

As part of the resource consent application, details of how the property will be carefully managed to avoid affecting neighbours must be supplied. The majority of our clients choose to have their holiday home managed by a professional property management agency, which we incorporate into the application. Whilst not a strict requirement, a property manager will assist you in meeting the conditions of your resource consent, in addition to promoting your property.

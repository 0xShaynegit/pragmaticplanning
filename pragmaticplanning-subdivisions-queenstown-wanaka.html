Subdivision Consents in Queenstown Lakes

From simple two-lot subdivisions to larger development projects, we provide strategic planning advice and coordinate the resource consent process through to approval. We work closely with surveyors, engineers and Council to help achieve successful outcomes.

[What You Need to Know]
  • All subdivisions in QLDC require resource consent before a surveyor can deposit a new plan
  • Minimum lot sizes vary significantly by zone. Some urban zones now allow lots under 300m2

Subdivision in Queenstown Lakes

Subdividing land in the Queenstown Lakes District is one of the most strategically complex planning activities in the country. The district's rapid population growth, combined with significant natural hazard constraints, Ngai Tahu cultural values, and infrastructure capacity limits, means that even straightforward-looking subdivision proposals require careful preparation.

The good news is that the Urban Intensification Variation (UIV) has opened up subdivision potential in many residential zones that were previously restrictive. Minimum lot sizes have reduced, density rules have relaxed, and the planning framework increasingly supports housing supply. For the right site, this is a significant opportunity.

The Subdivision Process

A standard subdivision in QLDC moves through the following stages:

  1. Feasibility assessment: We assess the site against the District Plan rules to confirm what subdivision potential exists, what consent is required, and what the realistic development yield is. This step saves significant time and money before any professional fees are committed.
  2. Resource consent application: We prepare and lodge the resource consent application, including the AEE and any specialist reports required. For larger subdivisions this may include geotechnical, traffic, servicing, and landscape assessments.
  3. Consent conditions: Once consent is granted, it will contain conditions covering engineering works, servicing requirements, esplanade reserves, and other matters. We review these carefully and advise on compliance obligations and timeframes.
  4. Engineering Acceptance (EA): QLDC must approve the engineering design for all subdivision works before construction begins. We coordinate between your engineer and Council to keep this process moving.
  5. Survey and title: Your surveyor prepares the survey plan. Once engineering works are complete and inspected, Council issues the Section 224(c) certificate, and Land Information New Zealand (LINZ) issues the new titles.

Zone-by-Zone Opportunities

Urban Intensification

Medium Density Residential Zone

The UIV has been transformative here. Minimum lot sizes have reduced and density controls have loosened significantly in the MDRAZ. Sites in Queenstown Hill, Frankton, and Wanaka South that were previously single-lot may now support two, three, or more lots. We have been working in this space since the UIV was notified and understand the Council's approach well.

Common Scenario

Low Density Residential Zone

Two-lot subdivisions are the most common application we handle. The key variables are site area, frontage, the ability to service both lots independently, and natural hazard status. In established residential areas the planning assessment is generally straightforward. In greenfield areas it can be more complex, particularly around stormwater and earthworks.

Rural

Rural and Rural Residential Zones

Rural subdivision in QLDC is tightly controlled. Minimum lot sizes are large, and the planning framework actively resists fragmentation of productive or open space land. However, there are pathways through boundary adjustments, lifestyle blocks, and clustering provisions. We assess each rural site individually and will give you an honest assessment of what is achievable.

Natural Hazards: The Critical Consideration

Queenstown Lakes sits within one of New Zealand's most hazard-prone regions. Flooding, erosion, and slope instability affect large areas of the district, and the District Plan requires careful assessment of hazard risk before subdivision consent can be granted.

QLDC uses the National Environmental Standards for Assessing and Managing Contaminants in Soil (NES-CS) and its own hazard overlay maps to identify at-risk sites. Any subdivision within a hazard area requires a geotechnical assessment from a suitably qualified engineer, and the Council's natural hazard risk policy must be addressed in the AEE.

We have handled subdivision applications involving all categories of QLDC's natural hazard overlays. Early identification of hazard constraints saves the cost of a full application only to find the site is not viable.

Infrastructure and Development Contributions

Every new lot in QLDC triggers development contributions (DCs). These are charges levied by Council to fund the infrastructure required to service new development, including water, wastewater, stormwater, roads, and community facilities. DC schedules are updated annually and the costs can be substantial, particularly for sites in Queenstown and Wanaka.

We factor DC costs into our feasibility assessments at the outset. For clients purchasing land with subdivision potential, understanding the DC liability before settlement is essential due diligence.

Common Questions

It depends on the zone. In the Low Density Residential Zone the minimum is typically 600m2, but site-specific factors can affect this. In the Medium Density Residential Zone the UIV has reduced minimums significantly in some areas. Rural zones have large minimums, often 4ha or more. We confirm the applicable minimum as the first step of any feasibility assessment.

From lodgement to consent decision, non-notified subdivision applications target 20 working days. The full process from initial advice to new titles typically takes 12-18 months for a standard two-lot subdivision, accounting for consent, engineering design, construction, inspection, survey, and LINZ processing. More complex subdivisions take longer.

Under the RMA, subdivision adjoining lakes, rivers, and the coast typically requires dedication of an esplanade reserve or strip.

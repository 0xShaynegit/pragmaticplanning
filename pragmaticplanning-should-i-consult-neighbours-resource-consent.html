Should I Consult With My Neighbours Before Applying for Resource Consent?

Two answers: the pragmatic one and the official one. Both point in the same direction. Here is why early neighbour consultation is almost always the right call before lodging a resource consent application in Queenstown Lakes District.

[TL;DR]
  • Consultation is not legally required but always recommended
  • Permitted activity = no objection pathway for neighbours
  • Resource consent: Council assesses if neighbours are genuinely affected
  • Neighbours sign affected party approval = Council ignores their effects
  • No consultation + adversely affected neighbour = limited notification risk
  • Early consultation = winning

When working alongside our clients we are often asked: "Do I need to consult with my neighbour and get their permission?" There are two answers to this question: the pragmatic answer, and the official answer.

The Pragmatic Answer: Yes

Of course you should consult with your neighbours when planning a development on your property.

Think of it this way: if your neighbour was planning to construct something on their land, even if it was allowed by the Planning Rules, would you prefer to find out over a coffee, or by the sudden appearance of construction workers?

We have seen early consultation with neighbours prevent many issues and frustrations before serious money has been spent on a development. It is much easier to change lines on paper than to relocate physical buildings.

[Real Example]

The Barn Shed That Almost Caused a Dispute

One of our clients was planning to construct a new barn-style shed in a particular location on their property. When discussing the project with their neighbour, they discovered that constructing the shed in that location would result in significant shading for the neighbour's sleepout. The client was happy to move the location of the shed just 10 metres. It made little difference to the overall success of the project. The result: a great functional shed and happy neighbours. Early consultation = winning.

Early consultation = less risk for the owner of the project.

We hear many clients say: "But if we tell our neighbours there is a higher chance they will object!"

Here are two facts worth knowing:

  • Fact 1: Neighbours can only object to the Council if your development requires resource consent. If you do not require a resource consent, it is not possible for them to officially object.
  • Fact 2: Even if you do require a resource consent, the Council will determine whether or not your neighbours are truly adversely affected to a noticeable degree. If not, you will likely be granted consent without needing to consult them further.

The Official Answer: Also Yes

Consultation prior to lodging a resource consent application is not mandatory under the Resource Management Act (RMA). However it is encouraged, and the RMA requires you to outline any consultation undertaken as part of your initial application.

If Your Activity Is Permitted

If your activity is permitted under the District Plan, you do not require a resource consent. This means there is no formal pathway for neighbours to object. If they are not happy with your project, there is no official process through which they can intervene. That said, a quick conversation can prevent a great deal of ill-will and future issues.

If You Require a Resource Consent

The Council will consider whether any person is adversely affected by your proposal to a noticeable degree. Even if your neighbour is objecting, if the Council determines your proposal will not affect them, consent is granted without the need for further consultation.

If you do the pragmatic thing and consult with your neighbours, and they are happy, you can ask them to sign an affected party approval form (a standard QLDC form) and a copy of the plans. When you submit this with the resource consent application, the Council will disregard all effects on that neighbour, meaning less report writing, a smoother process, and lower costs.

If you do not consult with your neighbours and simply lodge a resource consent application, the Council may determine that a person is adversely affected and ask for their written approval, or push the application down a limited notification path. This is not ideal.

Early consultation = winning.

Not automatically. Even if a neighbour is objecting, the Council will assess whether they are genuinely adversely affected to a noticeable degree. If the Council determines they are not, consent can be granted without their written approval. However, if a neighbour is found to be adversely affected and refuses to give written approval, the application may proceed via a limited notification or fully notified pathway, which is more complex and costly.

An affected party approval is a signed form (available from QLDC) where a neighbour or other potentially affected party gives their written consent to your proposed development. When submitted with a resource consent application, the Council will disregard all effects on that party when assessing the application. This simplifies the application, reduces report writing, and can lower cost and processing time.

Limited notification is a process where QLDC notifies specific parties (usually identified neighbours) of a resource consent application and invites them to make a submission. It is triggered when the Council determines that a person is adversely affected and written approval has not been obtained. Limited notification increases the complexity, cost, and processing time of the application compared to a non-notified consent.

QLDC makes building consent and resource consent information publicly accessible through the Edocs portal. You can search by the property's Assessment Number, which is found on the QLDC Mapping Viewer. See our guide on how to find out what your neighbours are planning.

Early consultation gives you the opportunity to address concerns before serious money has been spent. It is much easier to change lines on paper than to relocate physical buildings. A neighbour who feels respected and consulted is far less likely to raise a formal objection. And if they are not adversely affected, even if they object, the Council may still grant consent without their approval.

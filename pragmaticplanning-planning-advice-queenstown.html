In the high-stakes environment of Queenstown Lakes real estate, the most expensive mistake you can make is buying a problem. Good planning advice can save significant time, cost and risk. The Section 37 Red Card: The Developer's Silent Killer

The most common call we receive is from a property owner who has a builder ready to go, but a Section 37 notice has stopped the project cold. Under Section 37 of the Building Act, the Council is legally obligated to withhold a building consent if a resource consent is also required but has not yet been granted.

With the Urban Intensification Variation now in full effect, many owners assume their project is permitted as of right. If your design breaches even a minor 2026 standard, such as the new loading space requirement for suburban units or a subtle recession plane issue, your building consent will be flagged automatically.

The result: you pay interest on a construction loan for a site where you are not allowed to hammer a nail. We conduct a Section 37 Audit at the concept stage, ensuring your land use rights are clear before you engage an architect or commit to a construction contract.

Buying land in the Whakatipu Basin is no longer just about the view. Following the 2025 regional geological updates, the QLDC Hazard Register has been significantly revised. What this means in practice:

Ground Stability

New mapping in the Frankton Flats and Wanaka lakefront has reclassified several high-value areas. This impacts foundation design costs and insurance premiums. A site that looked straightforward two years ago may now require expensive ground improvement before building consent will be issued.

Alluvial Fan and Flood Risk

With the 2026 climate resilience standards, building near the 1-in-100-year flood line now requires a higher level of engineering freeboard, meaning minimum floor heights above ground level. This adds cost and can affect the buildable area of a site. We identify these constraints before they become problems.

Reverse Sensitivity: Airport and Industrial Zones

The Queenstown Airport Noise Mitigation Programme was updated in January 2026, setting strict new inner and mid-noise contour boundaries. If you build within these boundaries, you are legally required to provide acoustic insulation. This cost can be substantial if not identified during due diligence. Similarly, sites near the Frankton industrial zone carry reverse sensitivity obligations that affect both residential and commercial development.

The 2026 Granny Flat Trap

In early 2026, the government introduced a consent-free pathway for detached small dwellings up to 70m2. This received significant media coverage as a win for property owners. The reality on the ground in Queenstown Lakes is more nuanced.

The consent-free pathway applies to building consent, not resource consent. In QLDC, resource consent is still required in many situations where a new dwelling would breach site coverage, permeable surface ratios, or the residential flat standards in the District Plan. If your consent-free unit takes the site over its total coverage limit, the Council will issue an abatement notice requiring you to remove or modify the structure at your cost.

Additionally, QLDC's updated policy now applies development contributions to these smaller units to fund infrastructure. A bill of $15,000 to $20,000 before you connect the plumbing is not uncommon. We provide a plain-language assessment before any commitment is made.

National Policy vs. Local Reality

We are currently in a transitional period. The NPS-UD is pushing for growth, while the Planning Bill 2026 is rewriting the rules of engagement. For property owners and developers this creates both opportunities and traps.

The opportunities: national instruments increasingly override local QLDC restrictions in residential zones, and fast-track consenting pathways introduced in 2026 can bypass months of standard processing for qualifying projects. We identify where these pathways apply and how to use them.

The traps: the local rules that remain in place are more technical than ever. The Proposed District Plan Stage 3 decisions have introduced nuanced standards for urban design, outlook space, and interface treatment that catch projects out at the building consent stage after significant design investment. We check these against your concept before you pay for detailed drawings.

Strategic Reverse Sensitivity Protection

Reverse sensitivity is the planning term for the situation where a new activity is affected by an existing, lawful activity nearby. If you are developing a residential site near a commercial operation, a noise source, or an agricultural use, your new residents may later complain about that existing activity, creating pressure on the business or operation to change or cease.

We provide specialist advice on no-complaints covenants, acoustic buffering, and layout design that addresses reverse sensitivity issues at the planning stage rather than in dispute after construction. We also ensure applications address the mana whenua values of Kai Tahu, which if missed at the application stage can result in late appeals to the Environment Court at significant cost.
